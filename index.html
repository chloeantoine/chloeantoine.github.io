About

Welcome! I am a PhD student in economics at CREST — Institut Polytechnique de Paris, advised by Jean-Marc Bourgeon and Gregory Corcos. From January to July 2026, I visited the Harvard Kennedy School as a Fulbright Fellow, hosted by Wolfram Schlenker.

My research interests lie in the fields of environmental economics and international trade. I ask how trade and the policies that govern it affect biodiversity, and how nature in turn shapes what countries can produce and trade. To do so, I rely on both structural and empirical approaches, with a special enthusiasm for quantitative trade models and geospatial data.

Job market paper

  • Agriculture, trade and biodiversity
    FAERE Young Economist Best Paper Award 2026
    abstract
    paper
    Does agricultural trade liberalization erode biodiversity, or can it help preserve it? I build and estimate a new multicountry general-equilibrium model with explicit extensive and intensive margins of land use, in which biodiversity is both an endogenous outcome and a determinant of production. For birds, a standard indicator of ecosystem health, model counterfactuals show that trade preserves global biodiversity. It delivers land sparing at the scale of the trading system: on net, the habitat released saves more species than intensification costs. The liberalization of 1980 to 2017 spared about 26 bird species from eventual global extinction and averted some 2,200 local extinctions, in both cases about a tenth of what agriculture has committed. Undoing it would also cost farmers about $5.5 billion a year in farm revenue, through the ecosystem services birds provide. The gain is unevenly shared: biodiversity falls in a fifth of ecoregions. Restricting trade as a whole, however, would harm biodiversity overall. Mapping how extinctions respond to trade costs link by link shows, to first order, that a schedule protecting more biodiversity than current trade costs keeps most trade open, including every flow from rich temperate regions into the tropics, and concentrates restrictions on trade in animal products.

Working papers

  • Agricultural productivity and biodiversity effects: theory and evidence
    with Jean-Marc Bourgeon & José De Sousa
    R&R at Journal of Environmental Economics and Management
    abstract
    paper
    Agricultural specialization maximizes land use efficiency but it also amplifies pest pressure, thereby requiring heavy pesticide use. This paper evaluates the economic benefits of reducing specialization to leverage natural pest control. We develop a model in which the resilience of a farmer’s field is endogenous to the crop and pesticide choices of neighboring farmers. We account for both within-crop and between-crop cross-field externalities. Using global data on 40 crops and 39,116 counties, we estimate the impact of these externalities on crop productivity with instrumental variables constructed from agro-climatic suitability measures. Our results show that a one-percent increase in crop diversity raises crop yields by 1.3 to 1.8%, while a one-percentage-point increase in a crop’s own farmland share lowers that crop’s yield by 9 to 13%. Counterfactual simulations indicate that revenue-maximizing reallocation would roughly double the diversity of crops grown and that even a marginal reduction in specialization raises production.
  • Bounded by nature, limited by markets: the extensive margins of agricultural trade
    abstract
    request
    Climate change is projected to move the set of crops each country can grow, and how far trade can offset this depends on the fixed cost of entering new markets. I build a model in which soil, terrain and climate bound this set and fixed costs determine which feasible crops a country produces and where it sells them. Among trade agreements over 1992–2017, only the European single market raises the share of feasible crops exported to each partner, and its effect grows with the tariffs that accession removed. I estimate that halving the fixed cost of entering a market would raise entry about as much as removing a 12% tariff. Under climate projections, imports adjust, since a climatically able partner supplies a lost crop with an estimated probability of 75%. Exports adjust little: where potential shrinks, newly feasible crops seldom cover the fixed cost of a new market. Since tariffs deter entry elsewhere too, the countries that lose crops can raise that probability unilaterally, by removing their own tariffs on those crops.

Work in progress

  • Ricardo goes green: a quantitative spatial analysis of pesticide regulation's effects
    with Jean-Marc Bourgeon & José De Sousa
    abstract
    Globalization reshapes agricultural production not only by reallocating output across regions but also by intensifying the spatial concentration of crops. This paper develops a general-equilibrium model in which agricultural productivity is endogenously determined through ecological feedbacks. As trade integration lowers costs, countries expand cultivation of their comparative advantage crops, increasing land- share concentration. This concentration heightens pest pressure and reduces yields. Farmers respond by raising pesticide intensity, mitigating yield losses at the cost of greater environmental externalities. The model links trade-driven specialization, pesticide use, and productivity, allowing us to assess how environmental policies can propagate through international markets. In particular, the framework allows us to quantify the welfare and ecological impacts of tighter pesticide regulation in the European Union.
  • Maritime shipping and ocean biodiversity
    with Jeanne Astier
    abstract
    The environmental cost of shipping is usually counted in the carbon it emits, yet ships also impose local ecological costs that depend on where they sail, from nutrient deposition and physical disturbance to the transport of species between ports, and these costs are unpriced. Combining a routing model, in which trade volumes are given but paths are chosen, with satellite data on ocean productivity and 1.6 million container voyages, we study these costs and ask whether traffic should be concentrated into corridors or dispersed across them: land sparing versus land sharing, at sea.
